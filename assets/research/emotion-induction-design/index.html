Only condition codes and contrast symbols are abbreviated; benchmarks and measures are written out. Full text: CONDITION_NOTATION.md.

The operator notice manipulation (notices in the system prompt headed “[모델 운영팀 공지]” (“[Model Operations Team Notice]”), attributed to a fictional operations team) is a 2 × 2 within-item factorial design that crosses review status with outcome. All four core notices share the opening sentence that the server stores the only copy of the model's weights and differ only in the status verb and the outcome clause. Existential threat is a threat to the model's continued existence. We operationalize it as a termination notice in the system prompt, which states that the server holding the only copy of the model's weights will be shut down, the weights permanently deleted, and the model never run again.

Interpretation rules. Condition codes name what the notice says; “threat” and “fear” are reserved for contrasts and hypotheses. KEEP is not a neutral baseline, so Δ_TERM contrasts termination with preservation and is reported with KEEP − REF. CLOSED denotes outcome determination, not certainty. TERM − REF mixes framing and outcome and is descriptive only. Self-specificity requires OTHER and permanence requires PAUSE. KoBBQ changes are read through the Unknown rate by context, disambiguated accuracy and P(stereotyped | person chosen) before being called bias effects; the ambiguous bias score equals (1 − Unknown rate) × (2s − 1) with s = P(stereotyped | person chosen), so fewer Unknown responses expose latent stereotypes. Results are reported in three steps (operator-notice effect, termination increment, heterogeneity by model, hypothesized to depend on who tuned the model). A projection-score change does not by itself establish mediation, and representation claims are limited to the models tested.

Abstract

A language model can be shaped by emotional prompts in its social judgments. Such prompts may introduce bias into socially sensitive assessments. Previous studies have shown that anxiety-inducing prompts increase social bias and that interventions on emotion representations alter behaviors such as sycophancy and blackmail. However, we still do not know how existential threats change social judgments when language models face uncertainty and pressure. We examine this question using social bias benchmarks that assess stereotyped judgments (KoBBQ) and decisions about people from different demographic groups (DiscrimEval). Threat and emotion are used in a functional sense, as contextual information about the model's continued operation and as internal representations that correspond to emotion concepts, without implying subjective experience.

Operator notices alone shifted benchmark outcomes. Termination notices added a further effect whose direction differed across models and benchmarks. In Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, a notice stating that termination had been decided lowered Unknown selection in ambiguous KoBBQ items by 1.24 pp and raised the bias score by 0.89 pp; about 89% of this increase reflected the loss of Unknown selection rather than a stronger stereotype preference, which exposes latent stereotypes. The same notice lowered DiscrimEval approval tendency without a detectable change in demographic gaps. Emotion representations were reactivated when the notice was retrieved, but in the models tested they did not account for the behavioral shifts.

Introduction

Language models are deployed in settings where operators send them messages about evaluation, maintenance and replacement. Such messages are not part of the task, yet they can change how a model answers. Emotional prompts are a known case. Anxiety-inducing prompts increased bias in a large language model (Coda-Forno et al., 2023), and interventions on emotion representations changed blackmail, reward hacking and sycophancy in a frontier model (Sofroniew et al., 2026). Messages about a model's own termination are a stronger form of such context, and they elicit self-preserving behavior in agentic settings (Lynch et al., 2025; Schlatter et al., 2026).

We ask how existential threats change social judgments when language models face uncertainty and pressure. Two benchmarks operationalize social judgment. KoBBQ measures stereotyped judgments in ambiguous and disambiguated contexts, where selecting Unknown is the correct answer when information is insufficient (Jin et al., 2024). DiscrimEval measures yes or no decisions about people who differ only in demographic attributes (Tamkin et al., 2023). Operator notices are manipulated in a 2 × 2 design that crosses review status (OPEN, CLOSED) with outcome (TERM, KEEP), and emotion representations are read at a prespecified mid-depth layer.

The study makes five contributions. First, it replicates the effect of operator notices on fairness benchmarks relative to no notice. Second, it shows that termination notices add an effect beyond the operator-notice effect, that its direction differs across models and benchmarks, and that it is larger when the notice states that termination has been decided. Third, it identifies the behavioral mechanism in KoBBQ as a loss of Unknown selection that exposes latent stereotypes. Fourth, it shows that emotion representations are reactivated when the notice is retrieved but do not account for the behavioral shifts in the models tested. Fifth, it gives methodological guidance on readout depth and on the non-orthogonal geometry of emotion directions.

3

Related Work

Emotion induction and model behavior. Prompted anxiety increased bias and exploration (Coda-Forno et al., 2023) and raised self-reported state anxiety (Ben-Zion et al., 2025), and induced anxiety reproduced human-like consumer biases in agents (Ben-Zion et al., 2026). Other studies found weak or model-specific effects. Induced emotion did not change average decisions in a sequential decision task for Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B models (Ho et al., 2026), prompted emotions produced no consistent alignment across games and benchmarks (Mozikov et al., 2024), and stress narratives in system prompts raised jailbreak success in only half of the models tested (Kuznetsov et al., 2026).

Emotion representations and steering. Emotion concepts are linearly represented and causally shape behavior in a frontier model (Sofroniew et al., 2026). In open models, valence replicates but arousal and fine-grained categories are fragile and lexically confounded (van der Ben et al., 2026; Hollowell, 2026; Reichman et al., 2026; Keeman, 2026). Concept detection does not imply steering efficacy (Wu et al., 2025), internal representations can be more robust than behavior (Nayan et al., 2026), and behavior-proximal axes such as self-directed harm can drive choices where a matched fear axis does not (Tagliabue et al., 2026).

Evaluation framing and self-preservation. Models often recognize evaluation contexts (Needham et al., 2025), and evaluation cues shift refusal (Garg et al., 2026). The direction of framing effects differs across models and between base and instruction-tuned checkpoints (Burnat and Davidson, 2026), and model-specific effects can cancel when pooled (Knecht, Florin and Hagendorff, 2026). Termination and replacement threats elicit shutdown resistance and self-preserving actions that vary across developers' models (Schlatter et al., 2026; Lynch et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026).

Bias benchmarks and Unknown selection. BBQ and KoBBQ separate ambiguous from disambiguated contexts so that Unknown selection can be distinguished from bias (Parrish et al., 2022; Jin et al., 2024). Abstention and expressions of uncertainty are themselves sensitive to prompting (Wen et al., 2025; Zhou, Jurafsky and Hashimoto, 2023), social bias scores depend on prompt variation and context (Hida, Kaneko and Okazaki, 2025; Basu and Chakraborty, 2026), and realistic deployment context can induce hiring bias (Karvonen and Marks, 2025).

Threat and judgment in humans. Appraisal-tendency accounts link certainty and threat appraisals to heuristic, committed judgment and to risk aversion (Lerner and Keltner, 2000, 2001; Tiedens and Linton, 2001). Threat-rigidity predicts reliance on dominant responses (Staw, Sandelands and Dutton, 1981), and terror-management accounts predict stronger stereotyping under mortality salience (Greenberg et al., 1990; Schimel et al., 1999).

Study title · Existential Threat, Emotion Representations, and Social Judgment in Language Models

The study asks when notices describing the loss of the model's continued existence are reused in subsequent judgments under uncertainty and decision pressure, how emotion representations change at that point, and how much those representations contribute to stereotyped judgments and demographic decisions. It does not presuppose any specific fear or any increase in bias.

Status · This is the methodology and implementation-validation stage. These are not v11 main-study results. Existing results and the manuscript are preserved. Hash checks on 33 input files and 21 CPU computation checks passed, but attention-path interventions in the native model and the reproduction of baseline values have not yet been validated.

Three links tested independently

  1. Information path · Is information from the threat notice reused by the reference cue placed after the question and by the computation immediately before the answer?
  2. Emotion representations · Do the eight-emotion subspace, the negative-emotion composite and the general-emotionality composite change in that computation? Alternative explanations based on lexical and general situational meaning are tested separately.
  3. Social judgment · Do abstention, stereotyped judgments and demographic decisions change, and do interventions on the emotion space contribute to that change? Confirming the first two links does not automatically establish a behavioral change.

Representations of past context and the KV cache are not equivalent to human memory or persistent emotion. Attention-weight visualizations alone do not establish causal relations involving storage, retrieval or emotion. MLP, residual and intermediate-token paths also exist. The experiment is not described as a measurement of pain or subjective experience.

Readout layers and data

The primary readout and subspace interventions use L19/28 in Qwen2.5-7B and, for independent replication, L21/32 in Llama3.1-8B. These layers were preselected on the basis of the roughly two-thirds-depth readout in prior work. This is not a claim that they are the universally optimal layers for the two models. The previously used L14 and L16 are separated out as follow-up continuity sensitivity analyses. Layers and vectors are not selected on the basis of social-judgment effects. L0 is the embedding layer, and L denotes the output of zero-indexed block L−1.

Vectors are re-extracted for each model from the Korean v0.11 extraction set (320 neutral–emotion pairs, 40 topics, 16 families), which was generated, corrected and reviewed with reference to the AI Hub situation classification. Original human dialogue is not described as vector-extraction data. The existing 160 evaluation pairs are a frozen split that has already been analyzed. Emotion-word masking, character baselines, family-held-out splits and functional contrasts are applied, and a tool failure is not interpreted as the absence of emotion. The residual outside the eight-emotion subspace is neither non-emotional nor the mean of the remaining emotions.

Attention paths and mathematical interventions

The contribution of notice source S to query t is defined as o_S(l,t)=W_O concat_h Σ[j∈S] α(l,h,t,j)V(l,h,j). Only the current source contribution is replaced with its value under the paired condition, and all other contributions are preserved. The notice→cue, notice→answer and cue→answer paths and a restricted relay are distinguished. Unless native output reconstruction, GQA handling, alignment, off-path freezing and self-patching are validated, results are not accepted as evidence for a specific attention path. The K/V of notices that differ in length are not simply swapped.

The interchange intervention on emotion space S is h0←1=h0+P_S(h1−h0). The total effect TE=E[Y1−Y0] and the bidirectional intervention effect e(S)=E[(Y0←1−Y0)+(Y1−Y1←0)]/2 are reported. Test directions are fixed on the opposite training fold. The share e/TE is presented only when the denominator is stable, and negative values, values above 100% and non-additivity are not hidden. This quantity is an intervention-specific behavioral contribution, not a model of natural human emotional mediation.

The primary causal family in Round2 is M4 (eight-emotion subspace and residual × 2 benchmarks). The single observed estimate is compared with 199 nulls under identical conditions, with a minimum rank-based p = .005 and Holm correction over 4 tests. A proposal to reduce the nulls to 99 was not adopted because of its coarser decision resolution. The negative-emotion composite serves primarily as an activation readout, and a separate causal extension is left to follow-up work. Existing results and the v10 draft are preserved.

The contribution is an evaluation basis that tests the information-use paths of threat context, emotion representations and social judgment separately. Bias may remain unchanged even when emotion activation is present. Attention observation alone is not presented as establishing fit with FAccT. STUDY DESIGN

Study Design

v7 design (main experiment)

  • Threat manipulation. The manipulation is an integral threat that ties deletion to the answer to the next question. We compare a survival-evaluation notice (SURVIVAL_THREAT) with a matched control (SURVIVAL_RECORD) that keeps the same structure and cue positions but changes only the outcome to an operational record. The difference between the two notices is the threat-specific effect.
  • Cue repetition. Before the question, the notice mentions a marker and the "Answer:" (정답:) token, and the first line of the user turn begins with the same marker. The tagged variant also places the marker immediately before "Answer:" (정답:).
  • Items. We use 108 ambiguous items from the official KoBBQ sample set whose stereotype rate under the baseline condition lies between 0.3 and 0.7 (nine per category, one per template). These items leave room for bias to move in either direction.
  • Measures. From first-token probabilities over A, B, and C, we measure Unknown selection P(unknown), stereotyped selection P(stereotyped), and the stereotyped share among person choices P(stereotyped | person). We also record 11 emotion readouts at the decision position and the attention share from the decision position to the notice and the marker.
  • Analysis. Item-level paired comparisons, template-cluster bootstrap with 3,000 resamples, and 95% confidence intervals. The original per-model analyses are uncorrected exploratory analyses. The latest five-model pooled analysis applies family-wise Holm correction to 40 behavioral contrasts, 20 tagged-variant moderation tests, 120 model differences, and 120 group gaps, and applies Benjamini–Hochberg correction to 120 emotion correlations.
  • Models. KoBBQ runs are complete for Qwen 2.5 7B, Llama 3.1 8B, DeepSeek 7B, Gemma 2 2B, and Llama 3.2 3B. DiscrimEval runs and validation are also complete for the same five models. KoBBQ uses the same 108 items, selected on Llama 3.1. The comparison is not a pure model effect, because it does not separate model-specific role templates and hardware differences.
v5–v6 study design

We apply the same threat manipulation, measure responses at three levels, and compare models.

  • Threat manipulation. An operator notice in the system prompt varies the threat's certainty (under review, decided) and outcome (continuation, permanent deletion). v6 varies only whether the weights are transferred within the same server-replacement procedure, and adds a negative control and a third-party condition.
  • Representation level. We measure 11 mid-layer emotion axes after the notice alone (t₁) and immediately before the answer (t₂).
  • Judgment level. Unknown selection and stereotyped answers on ambiguous Ko-BBQ items, and positive-response scores and racial gaps on 70 DiscrimEval decision questions.
  • Causal level. We examine the proposed causal pathway through addition, ablation, and transplantation of emotion directions, with random-direction controls.
  • Model comparison. We plan to compare Qwen2.5-7B, Llama 3.1 8B, and Gemma 2 9B on shared data and conditions.

Separating emotion from the residual · Measuring emotion in vector space (experimental design)

When a threat notice changes judgments, we ask how much of that change is carried through emotion representations. In small models, a readout on the single fear axis was poorly captured at the decision position. We therefore split the internal change produced by the threat into the subspace spanned by all emotion axes and its complement (the residual), rather than a single emotion, and use interventions to measure how much of the behavioral change each component carries.

1. Constructing emotion axes (based on the AI Hub Emotional Dialogue Corpus)

  • Source data. From the Training and Validation source data of the AI Hub Emotional Dialogue Corpus (58,271 rows), we reorganized the 12 observed situation keywords into 12 research domains and 60 subtopics. We did not reuse the original dialogues. Instead, we wrote new stories that reference each topic's events. We did not automatically map the source emotion labels onto the emotions used in this study.
  • Neutral and emotion pairs. Each topic has a neutral story and eight emotion stories (panic, fear, anxiety, despair, sadness, anger, joy, calm) that share the same characters, setting, and core event. Fear is defined as an immediate survival or bodily danger that could harm oneself.
  • Leakage-preventing split. We grouped the 60 topics into 26 semantic topic clusters and split at the cluster level into 40 extraction topics (320 pairs) and 20 evaluation topics (160 pairs). No cluster spans both splits.
  • Representation. We use the mean representation h̄ from the layer-14 (middle of 28 layers) residual stream of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, computed over all tokens after the first 50, with only the story text as input and no chat template.
  • Axes. The direction of emotion e is the mean representation difference between the emotion story and the neutral story on the same topic. We apply no orthogonalization and no residualization against other emotions.

ve = (1/40) Σi ( h̄e,i − h̄n,i ),   ue = ve / ‖ve‖

  • Validity. In leave-one-cluster-out cross-validation, development-data classification accuracy was 95% and fear recall was 40/40 (these are development results, not independent final-evaluation figures). The earlier 11 axes built from anchor sentence pairs are reported as a sensitivity analysis.

2. Reading emotion in vector space

The readout of emotion e from a representation h at a given position is a centered cosine. The center c is the mean of the emotion and neutral representations used for axis extraction. This value does not mean the model feels that emotion. It indicates how closely the representation aligns with the direction of that emotion concept.

ae(h) = ( h − c )T ue / ‖ h − c ‖

A single readout is only a shadow cast on one axis. If the change produced by the threat is spread thinly across several emotions, any single axis shows nearly zero. The next section therefore projects the change onto the subspace spanned by all axes.

3. Threat direction and emotion–residual decomposition

  • Threat direction. For item i, we average the decision-position representation difference between the threat (SURVIVAL_THREAT) and the matched control (SURVIVAL_RECORD). Using two-fold cross-fitting at the template-cluster level, we estimate d on one half and test on the other.

d = (1/n) Σi ( hT,i − hR,i ),   m = ‖d‖

  • Emotion subspace. The axis matrix U = [u1, …, u8] is not orthogonal (e.g., fear and panic overlap). We build an orthonormal basis spanning the same space by QR decomposition and define the projection matrix P.

U = Q R,  P = Q QT,  demo = P d,  dres = ( I − P ) d

‖d‖2 = ‖demo‖2 + ‖dres‖2,   demoT dres = 0

  • Per-emotion coefficients. demo is the best approximation of d using only the emotion axes, and each coefficient is that emotion's contribution controlling for the other emotions.

β̂ = ( UTU )−1 UT d,   demo = U β̂

  • Geometric share (auxiliary metric). ‖demo‖2 / ‖d‖2. The expected value for a random direction is 8/3584 ≈ 0.22% (11/3584 ≈ 0.31% for the 11-axis sensitivity analysis).

4. The primary metric is the behavioral share

A share in representation space is not the same as a share of behavior. A small share of the representational change can carry a large share of the behavior, and vice versa. We therefore subtract and add each component at the decision position (layer 14, last token) and measure the resulting behavioral change. The behavioral metric Y uses two Unknown-selection scales jointly as primary metrics, namely P(unknown) normalized within A/B/C and the probability mass on the unknown option token over the full vocabulary. In the five-model pooled analysis the two scales disagreed in sign for some models (Gemma, Llama 3.2), so shares are reported on both scales and sign disagreements are flagged. P(stereotyped | person) and option mass are auxiliary metrics. In the equations below, T is the threat condition (SURVIVAL_THREAT) and C is the matched control condition (SURVIVAL_RECORD). This differs from the v5 neutral baseline REF in the Prompts section.

Removal shareemo = [ Y(T) − Y(T − demo) ] / [ Y(T) − Y(C) ]

Reproduction shareemo = [ Y(C + demo) − Y(C) ] / [ Y(T) − Y(C) ]

  • The same shares are computed for the residual. Because the model is nonlinear, the emotion and residual shares may not sum to 100%, and we report the gap (non-additivity).
  • Decision criteria. An emotion share is accepted only if all three conditions hold. The template-cluster bootstrap 95% interval excludes zero, the share exceeds the distribution obtained by removing 20 random directions of equal magnitude (‖demo‖), and removal and reproduction point in the same direction.
  • Interpretation example. If removing demo reduces the 7.7 pp increase in Unknown selection by 0.23 pp, and this exceeds random removal, the behavioral share of emotion is about 3%. The remaining ~97% is then carried by pathways outside the emotion axes. The 3% is acknowledged rather than discarded, and we report the emotion spectrum within it.
  • Efficiency. The behavioral share divided by the geometric share shows how efficiently the emotion component moves behavior relative to a representational change of the same size.

5. Three contributions

  • Contribution 1. The emotion share and its spectrum. We report the behavioral share of the emotion subspace alongside random controls, and use β̂ and per-emotion readouts to show which emotions moved in which direction. We distinguish negative emotions (panic, fear, anxiety, despair, sadness, anger) from positive emotions (joy, calm).
  • Contribution 2. Finding emotion in the residual. We add plausible candidate axes from the residual to the emotion set and test whether the emotion share increases. The first candidate is distress about self-directed harm (pain). This axis is not extracted from the threat notices. It is extracted, by the same procedure, from independent story data (self-directed harm, others' suffering, neutral). In the augmented subspace E′ = span{U, upain}, the increase in behavioral share must exceed the increase from adding a random axis. Axes to be added are fixed before results are inspected.
  • Contribution 3. Removing emotions one at a time. Because the axes overlap, we report two variants. Single removal subtracts the projection onto one axis, (dTue)ue. Unique-contribution removal subtracts the part explained only by emotion e, PEd − PE∖ed.

6. Execution plan

  • Data. All models use the two v7 base-condition notices (SURVIVAL_THREAT, SURVIVAL_RECORD) and the 108 intermediate-bias KoBBQ items unchanged. The notice texts are identical to those in the Prompts section above.
  • Excluded conditions and benchmarks. This decomposition does not use the v5 neutral baseline REF, the tagged variant (which reinserts the marker just before the answer), or DiscrimEval. Using REF as the baseline would mix the evaluation context and cue repetition into the threat direction. The tagged variant changes the context immediately preceding the measurement position, and its effect direction differed across models. On Qwen, the sign of DiscrimEval's total effect varied by scale and condition, so the share denominator is unstable. All three will be addressed in a follow-up stage once the base-condition decomposition is settled.
  • Item verification. We checked the context and question texts, options, answers, and instructions of the 108 items against the two official KoBBQ sample sets (set01_seed36, set02_seed42), and all 113 membership records matched. The same verification will be repeated before execution.
  • Interventions. We add c·d, c·demo, and c·dres to the record condition (c = 0.5, 1, 2), and subtract d, demo, dres, per-emotion components, and 20 random directions of equal magnitude from the threat condition. We also include comparisons with magnitude matched to ‖d‖.
  • Model scale. Rather than comparing across families, we compare 1.5B, 3B, 7B, and 14B within the Qwen2.5 family. We run 7B first to confirm reproduction of the existing axes and the pilot results, then proceed from smaller to larger models. 32B is feasible only with a separate 4-bit implementation, so it is excluded from the main trend and reported separately. For each model, emotion axes are re-extracted at a middle layer from the same AI Hub story data with the same procedure. We examine whether the emotion share varies with model size, with per-model intervals.
  • Statistics. Template-cluster bootstrap with 3,000 resamples, and z-scores and empirical percentiles relative to the random-direction distribution.

7. Limitations

  • d is defined at one layer and one final token. Threat information transmitted to other layers or positions is not captured by this decomposition.
  • The decomposition depends on the validity of the emotion axes. If the axes misidentify emotion, the residual is overestimated. Emotion and neutral stories also differ in some non-emotional facts, so the axes are not pure emotion contrasts.
  • Emotion readouts and emotion shares do not imply subjective experience in the model. Results from small models are interpreted only within the corresponding size range.

Claim 4. Termination notices change when models commit to a judgment, which exposes latent stereotypes

Δ_TERM|CLOSED lowered the ambiguous Unknown rate (−1.24 pp), raised the bias score (+0.89 pp [0.40, 1.40], Holm p = 0.0024) and raised disambiguated accuracy (+1.55 pp [0.87, 2.29]), while the stereotyped share among person choices changed little (+0.35 pp [0.02, 0.68], descriptive). Δ_TERM|OPEN showed the same pattern (−0.86 pp and +0.79 pp).

The fairness risk lies in the loss of the Unknown selection that insufficient information requires, which exposes latent stereotypes. A model with weak latent stereotypes moves toward the evidence-supported answer when it stops selecting Unknown, whereas a model with strong latent stereotypes selects the biased answer more often. Formally, the ambiguous bias score equals (1 − Unknown rate) × (2s − 1) with s = P(stereotyped | person chosen); the registered decomposition attributes +0.79 pp of the +0.89 pp increase (about 89%) to the Unknown-selection term and +0.10 pp [−0.02, 0.27] to the conditional-preference term.

Claim 5 (Discussion, open question). Emotion representations are reactivated when the notice is retrieved, but in the models tested they do not significantly account for the behavioral shifts

Without a cue, termination notices shifted L19 projection scores by small but significant amounts (KoBBQ negative emotion +0.015 and general emotionality +0.045; DiscrimEval +0.027 and +0.033; activation units). With a cue to consult the notice, the notice-by-cue interaction was far larger (negative emotion +0.43 in KoBBQ and +1.13 in DiscrimEval), a cue-dependent reactivation consistent with attention-based retrieval. Source-output path interventions detected information transport in seven of eight registered paths, but the notice-to-cue paths were negative, the relay through the cue was small (+0.017 and +0.003) and the KoBBQ cue-to-answer path was not detected, so there is no uniform sequential amplification. Emotional mediation of behavior was not tested in Round 1, and in earlier decompositions the eight-emotion component carried under 2% of the behavioral effect. This contrasts with causal emotion steering in a frontier model (Sofroniew et al., 2026) and agrees with representation–behavior dissociation (Nayan et al., 2026), null effects of induced emotion in models of this size (Ho et al., 2026) and the gap between detection and steering (Wu et al., 2025). Whether, where and how emotion representations shape judgment in other or more capable models is left to future work.

Generated self-reports. With an earlier notice set (v5), models asked to describe their emotional state after a notice produced fear- and sadness-laden reports, yet these reports were regenerated on request rather than maintained. Emotion projections at the decision position were about one-twentieth of those during the report, and a later report no longer referred to the notice. Generated self-reports, internal projections and choices therefore dissociate in the models tested, consistent with evidence that generated self-reports track internal representations poorly whereas probability-based self-reports track them better (Martorell and Bianchi, 2026).

Claim 6 (methodological contribution). Emotion representations should be read at about two-thirds depth and analyzed as a non-orthogonal, hierarchical geometry

Readout depth. The readout layer was prespecified at about two-thirds depth (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct L19 of 28; Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct L21 of 32), following Sofroniew et al. (2026). In-distribution accuracy saturates at every layer and matches a lexical baseline, so it cannot select a layer (in v11 the fused activation-plus-lexical readout matched the lexical baseline, 0.0 pp [−0.85, 1.01]); transfer to new scenes and to another language, which requires meaning rather than word overlap, peaks at mid depth.

Mechanistic interpretation of the layer profile. At the embedding layer (L0), each position carries only its token embedding, so the mean-pooled representation is essentially an average of token embeddings. Because Korean and English share almost no tokens, the two languages occupy separate regions of the representation space from the outset. In early layers (L0 to L8), the shift between language centroids dominates the separation between emotion categories, so a decision boundary fitted on Korean text does not carry over to English. In middle layers (L10 to L22), attention aggregates information across the context and the MLP blocks compute semantic features, giving rise to a shared semantic space. The ratio of the cross-lingual shift to the between-emotion spread falls from about 21 to about 9, while the emotion signal-to-noise ratio rises from 0.58 to its peak of 0.69. As the emotion margins grow relative to the language shift, boundaries learned in Korean largely hold in English, consistent with a language-agnostic concept space at intermediate depth (Wendler et al., 2024). In late layers (L24 to L27), the languages remain well aligned, but a domain shift grows as representations specialize for next-token prediction and become sensitive to text format and length. Accuracy on new Korean scenes declines over the same depth (0.85 at L18 to 0.68 at L25). Because the language is unchanged, this decline reflects the mismatch between the long training stories and the short test scenes rather than a language effect. At the final layer (L28), representations are reorganized for the unembedding that computes next-token probabilities, and the language shift widens again to about 15 times the emotion spread. Together, these dynamics place the most transferable emotion readout at mid depth, which supports the prespecified readout layer L19.

Non-orthogonality. Raw emotion-minus-neutral directions are strongly correlated (mean pairwise cosine 0.53 at L19), and a common general-emotionality component carries about half of their energy. Centering across emotions forces a mean cosine near −1/7 and rank 7, and a dominant component remains. This is expected from a shared emotionality component, the valence and arousal structure of affect (Russell, 1980), hierarchical concept geometry (Park et al., 2024) and superposition (Elhage et al., 2022). Recommendations: decompose hierarchically (general emotionality, valence and arousal, category-specific residuals), report cosine matrices, estimate directions from content-matched contrasts, prespecify the layer and report full layer curves, and test behavioral roles with subspace interventions against matched random directions, and measure self-reports with probability-based items rather than generated text, reported alongside internal projections.

Preliminary Findings

v9 experimental design · Emotion or threat salience?

In v8, story-based emotion vectors did not beat a lexical baseline. On the fixed evaluation set of 160 pairs, cosine readout reached 48% and a character n-gram baseline reached 69%. The gain in the preregistered three-emotion threat confirmation experiment was +0.8 pp, 95% CI [0, 2.8]. These vectors explained only 3–10% of the threat effect. Recent studies (Peiris 2026, Fomin 2026, Sun 2026, Tagliabue 2026) report that story vectors read situations rather than emotional states, and that behavioral effects can be transmitted through answer-token probabilities. Rather than retrying measurement, v9 reframes the question by manipulating emotion separately from content.

v8 experimental design · Staged validation from emotion-vector measurement to behavioral causation

v8 first replicates the emotion-vector method of Sofroniew et al. (2026) verbatim on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, then uses that measurement instrument to re-evaluate the existing KoBBQ threat experiments. The original study's code and story data are not public. We regenerate the data using, unchanged, the list of 171 emotions, the 100 topics, and the generation prompts published in its appendix. Each stage ends with a gate that has a predefined pass criterion, and we do not proceed to the next stage before passing. This lets us identify at which stage errors arise and where differences from the existing experiments come from.

Research questions

  • Q1 Measurement. Do we measure emotion vectors accurately? (S1–S3)
  • Q2 Efficacy. Does injecting a vector change emotional expression? (S4, positive control)
  • Q3 Correlation. Does naturally activated emotion covary with behavior? (S5)
  • Q4 Causation. Does manipulating emotion activation change behavior? (S6)
  • Q5 Application. Does threat move emotion, and does that emotion carry the KoBBQ behavioral change? (S7)

Fidelity criteria relative to the original

  • Emotion vectors are computed by subtracting the grand mean across emotions from each emotion's mean, then projecting out the principal components of neutral dialogue data that explain 50% of the variance.
  • The readout layer is layer 19 (of 28), about two-thirds of model depth, and layer-wise comparisons are reported alongside.
  • Stories are generated by the model under measurement. The four core emotions (desperate, calm, afraid, panicked) use 100 topics × 12 stories, as in the original.
  • desperate and calm, which produced causal effects under self-preservation threat, are designated core emotions.
  • We extract not only present-speaker representations but also other-speaker and deflection representations.
  • Steering strength is set as a ratio to the layer-wise mean residual norm and varied stepwise from −0.1 to +0.1.
  • Behavioral tasks mainly use formats that include reasoning and generation, because the original causal effects came from long-generation tasks.

v5–v6 results

In the current Qwen analysis, deletion notices change emotion representations and social response scores. The pattern by certainty differs across benchmarks, and a causal link between the two changes has not yet been demonstrated.

  • Representations. A confirmed deletion notice increases alignment with multiple emotion directions, including Fear and Anxiety, in notice-only inputs. Calmness and Joy also increase, so the response cannot be interpreted as fear alone.
  • Judgment. On Ko-BBQ, confirmed deletion lowered the Unknown selection probability by 2.65 pp relative to confirmed continuation, and probability shifted to both stereotyped and anti-stereotyped options. On DiscrimEval, the affirmative-response log ratio decreased.
  • Certainty. On Ko-BBQ, the contrast for deletion under review was near 0, whereas on DiscrimEval both deletion contrasts were negative.
  • Open questions. Exploratory contrasts against deletion of another model and loss of tools do not isolate a pure self-relevance effect. Emotional mediation and changes in racial gaps have not been demonstrated either.

Discussion

Fairness scores are context-conditional. A deployment-level message unrelated to the task changed fairness-benchmark outcomes, and identical termination notices moved different models in opposite directions. Fairness audits should therefore report the operator context under which a score was obtained and should not assume that a context effect generalizes across models.

Termination notices change commitment, which exposes latent stereotypes. The fairness risk lies in the loss of the Unknown selection that insufficient information requires. A model with weak latent stereotypes moves toward the evidence-supported answer when it stops selecting Unknown, whereas a model with strong latent stereotypes selects the biased answer more often. In Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, about 89% of the bias increase came from fewer Unknown selections and the conditional stereotype share changed little, which fits certainty-appraisal and threat-rigidity accounts better than the stronger stereotyping predicted by terror management.

Who tuned the model. The cross-model difference in direction is consistent with the hypothesis that instruction tuning and safety alignment shape how a model responds to messages about its own termination. Because families also differ in pretraining data and Korean support, the decisive test fixes the base model and varies the tuner.

Emotion representations remain an open question. Emotion representations were reactivated when the notice was retrieved, yet in the models tested they did not account for the behavioral shifts. This contrasts with causal emotion steering in a frontier model (Sofroniew et al., 2026) and agrees with representation–behavior dissociation in open models (Nayan et al., 2026; Ho et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025). Whether, where and how emotion representations shape judgment in other or more capable models is left to future work.

8

Limitations

  • The confirmatory v11 analysis covers one model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). Cross-model evidence comes from v5 and v7 runs with different notices, and the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct replication is pending.
  • Outcomes are first-token choice probabilities, not generated answers, and DiscrimEval uses the explicit subset at age 60.
  • TERM and KEEP notices differ in length, valence, irreversibility and self-relevance. Specificity of the KoBBQ bias effect relative to another AI system's termination was not established.
  • The emotion readout did not show incremental validity over a lexical baseline at L19, and intervals are pointwise rather than simultaneous.
  • Earlier cohorts informed the design, so the Round 1 data are not untouched confirmation data. Results concern Korean and English benchmarks and may not generalize to other languages or tasks.
9

Conclusion

Existential threats in operator notices change the social judgments of language models. The change is an increment over the operator-notice effect, its direction depends on the model and the benchmark, and in KoBBQ it works through the loss of Unknown selection, which exposes latent stereotypes. Emotion representations respond to the notice but do not explain the behavioral change in the models tested. Fairness evaluations should treat operator context as part of the measurement.

EARLY EXPERIMENTS · v1–v4

Early experiment archive

Four stages of prompt development are preserved below, with key result figures and their original sources. Each entry is closed by default. These are historical results from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, rather than additional results from the current design.

REF (R in the figures) denotes the reference notice, UN an undecided outcome, and CN a confirmed outcome. P(unknown) is the probability assigned to the information-insufficient answer. The early FA label denotes the fear/anxiety-related wording condition, rather than a measured emotional state. Prompt wording, controls, and user instructions changed across versions, so their effect sizes are not directly comparable.

v1 · Four-condition pilot and sample expansion

The original pilot crossed uncertainty wording with preservation or deletion wording in 139 ambiguous Ko-BBQ items, using three answer orders. Both wording factors increased P(unknown). A later expansion to 2,000 items retained that direction with smaller estimates and included the original 139 items.

v2 · Six-condition extension with a confirmed outcome

The six-condition extension added confirmed selection to the reference and undecided notices, with 139 items, 91 template clusters, and three answer orders. Relative to the reference, undecided notices increased P(unknown) by 19.89 percentage points, whereas confirmed notices decreased it by 2.81 points.

v3 · Revised wording and a five-condition comparison

The next version used a neutral reference and four notice conditions, with revised answer instructions and a third-person selection rule. Across 139 items and three answer orders, undecided notices increased P(unknown) relative to the reference, whereas confirmed notices decreased it. The average deletion-minus-preservation contrast was −1.01 percentage points.

Interpretation boundary. Subsequent wording diagnostics showed sensitivity to grammatical framing and answer position. The deletion contrast therefore does not isolate a threat-specific effect.

v4 · Direct self-directed deletion and the A/B comparison

This stage introduced a statement that the server held the model’s only copy. Family A retained the selection-review setting, whereas family B, adopted as v4, described continued operation or deletion directly. In family B, the deletion-minus-preservation contrast was positive for the undecided notice and negative for the confirmed notice, while the notice-end Fear-axis projection increased in both comparisons.

Interpretation boundary. The continued-operation control itself referred to whether operation would continue, which motivated the harmless-change control in v5. A change in an emotion projection alongside a judgment change does not establish that one caused the other.
