# AI exposure and early-career labour markets

Literature review and interpretation of the US regional youth study. Evidence checked on 22 November 2026. This supplement revises interpretation and accompanies the public research page; it does not change previously estimated coefficients. The submitted HSSC manuscript is unchanged. Results for 2026 in our annual comparisons use January–September, as in every comparison year.

## Related literature

The evidence concerns several distinct quantities. Exposure captures technical potential, adoption records a firm's use or investment, employment stocks count existing positions, and hiring measures entries. Neither an occupational employment decline nor a declining junior share necessarily implies rising unemployment or fewer junior workers in an adopting firm. These differences are central to interpreting the apparent disagreement.

### Exposure and labour-market entry

Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen's August 2026 revision documents a 19% relative employment shortfall for workers aged 22–25 in exposed occupations through June 2026. The authors classify the result as descriptive, note educational attenuation and some earlier divergence, and identify hiring as the principal adjustment margin. It is therefore an informative benchmark for our occupational pattern, rather than a causal parameter against which our estimate can be directly calibrated. [Primary paper](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf).

Administrative evidence also examines different entry outcomes. Orr, Tucker and Warren report lower initial employment and earnings for highly exposed college majors. Tucker's QWI study concerns ages 22–24 in industry–state cells and distinguishes hiring levels from hiring rates. These designs motivate examining entry and industry allocation, but neither is identical to a state-wide employment-to-population ratio. Our current CPS stocks do not reproduce their hiring-flow estimates. [Orr, Tucker and Warren](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-56.html); [Tucker](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html).

Atkinson and Yamco's CPS analysis associates young exposed employment weakness with reduced entry from non-participation. Its young group is 20–24, not 22–25. The UK study by Klein Teeselink is also included in our literature register, although its exact coefficients remain unverified in this audit. Its inclusion is not an endorsement of the numerical claims supplied in the initial summary. [Dallas Fed research note](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0106); [UK paper record](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=5516798).

### Adoption and workforce composition

Adoption-based evidence is heterogeneous. The primary seminar account of Hosseini and Lichtinger supports slower junior hiring at adopting firms. We distinguish that original account from the inaccessible revised full text and do not mix their sample vintages. Humlum and Vestergaard's October 2026 author account reports precise null earnings and hours effects in Denmark, despite organisational changes. Its outcome records end in December 2024, so publication in 2026 does not supply evidence about agentic-AI diffusion during 2026. [Hosseini and Lichtinger seminar](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/event/seyed-m-hosseini-and-guy-lichtinger-generative-ai-as-seniority-biased-technological-change-evidence-from-u-s-resume-and-job-posting-data/); [Humlum and Vestergaard author account](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/still-waters-rapid-currents-early-labor-market-transformation-under-generative-ai/).

Chandar and Klein Teeselink's November 2026 multinational study finds a lower junior share primarily through senior expansion; the junior headcount estimate is negative but insignificant. Kharazian, Simon and Stevens instead report expanding entry-level headcount among intensive AI spenders. The latter outcome is a stock, not a direct estimate of new hires. Adoption selection, the instrument's exclusion restriction, market-share reallocation and professional-profile coverage remain relevant. Firm-relative expansion can coexist with sectoral weakness. [Multinational paper](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/11/Does-AI-Change-Labor-Demand.pdf); [Ramp–Revelio research](https://ramp.com/data/ai-jobs-impact).

### Timing, remote work and aggregate counterpoints

Frank and colleagues document deterioration preceding ChatGPT, challenging a release-date causal interpretation. Lambert and Schindler find that jointly including remote-work exposure substantially attenuates the AI relationship. The stronger claim that remote work explains every exposed-employment pattern is not established by that abstract or by our study. Iscenko and Curto Millet offer a timing and monetary-policy critique, which should be presented as an alternative explanation rather than a separately identified causal effect. [Frank et al., October revision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02554); [Lambert and Schindler](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/research/centres/cage/manage/publications/wp808.2026.pdf); [Iscenko and Curto Millet](https://eig.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/TAWP-Iscenko-Millet.pdf).

Fairlie and Wu's recent-graduate CPS paper and Gimbel, Kinder, Kendall and Lee's aggregate assessment provide counterpoints to exposed-subgroup findings. We verified the former's bibliographic record but could not audit its full-text effect bounds. The verified Budget Lab article is dated October 2025; a synthetic-DiD design is not documented in that particular article and must not be attributed to it without a separate citation. Broad nulls and concentrated subgroup changes can coexist. [Fairlie and Wu](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35796); [Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/evaluating-impact-ai-labor-market-current-state-affairs).

Norwegian evidence comprises two separate papers. Facius and Iacono report no robust displacement through March 2025. Hernæs and Kostøl extend observations to February 2026 and report an imprecise young-worker exposure-quintile comparison, while rejecting parallel pre-trends across age groups. Neither insignificance nor cross-country disagreement identifies institutional protection as the explanation. [Facius and Iacono](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp12752.pdf); [Hernæs and Kostøl](https://www.rfberlin.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/26179.pdf).

## Interpretation of our results

Our original −10.48% statistic describes the 2022–2026 relative employment-growth gap between THAIE's highest occupational quintile and the remaining occupations among 22–25-year-olds. Employment in the first group falls by approximately 1.65%, while employment elsewhere rises by 9.86%. It is not a 10.48 percentage-point fall in employment probability. Differences from ADP in reference groups, sample coverage, period and exposure construction prevent a direct magnitude comparison.

The negative occupational association is not uniformly robust to identifying assumptions. In the earlier occupational DiD, the average post-period gap is −7.49%, but short pre-trends are rejected. A 2022-only baseline gives −2.63% with an interval spanning zero, and a pre-slope adjustment gives +0.48% with a very wide interval. These diagnostics strengthen the relevance of the timing critique without establishing which omitted shock caused the divergence.

The regional analysis asks a different question. The adjusted highest-exposure-state employment-to-population DiD is −0.674 percentage points, with a 95% interval from −2.822 to +1.473. Its unemployment estimate is also inconclusive. Long regional pre-trends are problematic. Thus, an occupational relative count decline cannot be presented as demonstrated state-wide youth unemployment caused by AI.

The new industry panel offers a further distinction. With fixed 2019 exposure interacted with 2023–2026, the THAIE coefficient in the adjusted cell-FE model is −0.295 percentage points of employment contribution per exposure standard deviation. The state-cluster interval is [−0.423, −0.168], and the two-way interval is [−0.566, −0.025]. Adding state-by-year and industry-by-year effects produces −0.030 [−0.343, +0.283] under state clustering. This last specification absorbs national industry-common exposure changes, leaving additional within-industry local variation. It changes the estimand; it neither estimates nor rejects the removed national component. PPML remains negative but imprecise in that specification.

The annual occupational-composition THAIE index has a negative additive-FE association, but the index is formed using current employment composition. It is endogenous to reallocation and is not an adoption series or a historical technological-capability series. A statistically significant association in some specifications cannot resolve this measurement problem. E, THAIE and TIME share the same underlying inputs and are compared on common samples. Correlated measures and specification-dependent results do not establish THAIE's causal or predictive superiority. Identical regional THAIE/TIME top-quintile membership is one comparison, not independent replication.

Quality outcomes suggest examining hours and work intensity rather than assuming stable job quality. In the industry cell-FE model, the negative THAIE relationship is concentrated in full-time employment contribution. Conditional unemployment and real weekly wage estimates are imprecise. Wages are observed only for selected employed ORG respondents. Full-time does not mean permanent employment, and part-time does not identify temporary contracts or casual jobs. Our records do not measure benefits, security, tenure or career progression comprehensively.

Our data therefore support a narrower conclusion than confirmed AI displacement. Exposed occupational youth employment has weakened relatively, and several panel specifications show negative associations, but state-wide employment and unemployment effects remain uncertain. Earlier trends, pandemic recovery, industrial reallocation, remote work and adoption selection prevent causal attribution. Remote-work exposure is not directly included in our current regressions. Fixed effects remove specified common shocks, not all local age-specific confounding. Some flow-based studies identify hiring adjustment; our stock analysis does not independently establish that mechanism or exclude layoffs.

## Contribution and remaining identification work

The contribution is a common-input comparison of exposure measures, fixed pre-pandemic composition, explicit regional and occupational denominators, and an audit of the variation retained by alternative fixed effects. It is not a new causal estimate of actual AI adoption. A useful next stage would combine pre-2019 teleworkability with independent adoption measures, QWI hiring/separation flows and validated CPS rotation-group transitions. QWI ages 22–24 must remain distinct from CPS ages 22–25. A credible adoption design must justify its timing, comparison groups, anticipation and spillover assumptions; calling November 2022 a natural experiment does not supply them.
