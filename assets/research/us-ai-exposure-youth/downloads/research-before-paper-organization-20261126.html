AI, labor, and social policy

AI exposure, entry opportunities, and job quality in the United States

Core research question

Does weaker full-time employment among young workers in highly AI-exposed fields reflect fewer hiring opportunities, poorer offered job quality, or both? Who experiences these changes?

We examine worker adaptation across the age distribution, with particular attention to labor-market entrants and workers facing economic vulnerability.

Compare pooled state employment, monthly occupational trends, entry into work, and the experiences of incumbent workers. Each source answers a different question.

Updated November 26, 2026 · CPS, ACS, SIPP, and public administrative data · Own THAIE mapping

Employment among adults aged 22–25 grew less in the highest THAIE quintile than in lower-exposure occupations. Aligned CPS stock–flow accounting reproduces relative full-time weakness but does not identify a specific entry, exit, schedule, occupation, or aging pathway. Controlled entry models do not establish an additional Q5 decline. Attrition assumptions and sizable survey/coverage reconciliation limit mechanism attribution; the findings do not establish an AI causal effect.

Introduction

Generative AI could change both the number of entry opportunities and the quality of jobs offered. Employment stocks alone cannot distinguish reduced hiring from separations, aging, occupational reallocation, or individual moves into part-time and contingent arrangements. This study combines those outcomes only where their data and denominators support comparison.

We independently reconstruct monthly employment from official Basic Monthly CPS microdata and assign occupations using our own THAIE methodology.

Related evidence and alternative explanations

Canaries in the Coal Mine documents exposed early-career employment weakness. Generative AI at Work demonstrates productivity gains in a specific firm setting. Adoption studies and broader labor-market assessments do not uniformly find displacement. Pre-existing trends, pandemic recovery, remote work, monetary conditions, and firm selection remain competing explanations.

Methods

We independently aggregate official CPS employment counts and link occupations to our frozen THAIE classification. Six exact age groups are retained. Employment indices use November 2022 = 100 and display November 2022 onward; they are not employment rates or causal effects.

Employment-index charts display November 2022 onward. DID validation retains the pre-period from December 2018 and uses symmetric windows around the excluded November 2022 transition month. The 36-month window is a robustness check. Regressions use observed months only.

Full data construction, support and estimation methods

Population and age groups. CPS estimates cover employed civilian noninstitutional people aged 16 and older in the 50 states and DC. The six focal groups are 22–25, 26–30, 31–34, 35–40, 41–49, and 50+. The all-worker series also includes ages 16–21. Employed people include those at work and those temporarily absent. Composite final weights produce employment counts, not employment-to-population rates. Full-time and part-time follow the CPS labor-force classification and do not identify permanent versus temporary contracts. Recorded sex is reported as women and men.

Exposure. Occupational THAIE quintiles use frozen 2019 employment-based membership. Quintiles are not recalculated within age, sex, or employment-status groups. Scores are retrospective task-based measures, not observed employer adoption. Unmapped occupations remain unassigned.

Current symmetric-window DID and event-study design

The primary pre-period is December 2018–October 2022 and the post-period is December 2022–October 2026. November 2022 is excluded as the transition month. Each side spans 47 calendar months, with 47 observed pre-months and 46 observed post-months because October 2025 is unavailable. The 36-month robustness window uses November 2019–October 2022 and December 2022–November 2025, with 36 and 35 observed months. No interpolated value enters a regression. Earlier 2017-start specifications remain explicitly archived.

Age-specific Poisson quasi-likelihood models estimate Q5 versus Q1–Q4 contrasts in weighted full-time or part-time counts. Fixed effects are occupation×industry, occupation×month-of-year, and industry×calendar-date. Separate Q5 interactions cover pandemic quarters in 2020–2021. Earlier occupational codes are probabilistically bridged to the common classification. Industry controls do not remove every occupation-specific or local shock.

Main FT and PT models share supported rows within each age and window. For ages 22–25 in the long window, this retains 91.75% of weighted FT and 99.48% of PT employment. FT-only sensitivity restores all covered FT employment. Zero survey counts in supported model cells are not administrative proof of zero jobs. The descriptive chart threshold is not imposed on estimation.

Reported intervals use occupation-score cluster covariance and are working model-based intervals, not complete CPS survey-design intervals. The 12 primary age-by-outcome contrasts receive Holm correction. Event studies use monthly observations in three-month relative-time bins, with August–October 2022 as the omitted reference. Pandemic quarters have separate nuisance coefficients and are excluded from the flat-lead joint test. Intervals are pointwise rather than simultaneous; boundary bins can contain fewer months.

Population controls, rotation overlap, historical crosswalk error, retrospective exposure, support selection, and unobserved AI adoption remain limitations. Numerical reproduction does not establish parallel trends or a causal AI effect. The current results and their sensitivities are reported together rather than selected by statistical significance.

The opening map pools observed CPS months into three-year calendar windows and reports Q5 full-time employment divided by all same-age residents. The independent ACS check compares 2017–2019 with 2023–2024 and uses past-year usual-hours proxies. Linked CPS separates entry outcomes from incumbent transitions; SIPP observes personal histories through 2024. These distinct measures are not pooled into one causal estimate.

New survey and administrative validation methods

ACS validation uses 2017, 2018, 2019, 2023, and 2024 national person files, exact ages 22–25, all states/DC, frozen THAIE thresholds, and 80 replicate weights. The 2017 occupation bridge contributes fractional expected group weights. Three-year pre and two-year post averages use separately normalized annual weights. Pointwise intervals assume independent annual replicate blocks and condition on the score and bridge. ACS hours and salary refer to the past year, not the current CPS schedule or hourly wage.

The earlier descriptive linked-CPS module uses 79 genuine adjacent-month pairs from 2020–October 2026, rotation eligibility, validated person matches, and cross-fitted response IPW with trimming sensitivity. Origin occupation defines incumbent exposure; destination occupation classifies entry outcomes from a common nonemployment denominator. Neither unknown prior occupation nor missing response is coded as low exposure. The pooled transition periods differ in length and include pandemic effects, so they are descriptive rather than controlled DID estimates.

SIPP uses official two-, three-, and four-year longitudinal weights. Monthly hours intensity is distinguished from usual FT/PT, and general permanent-to-contract status is not inferred from union coverage or restricted-universe contract questions. QWI/J2J use quarterly industry exposure and published ages 22–24/35–64, not exact occupational Q5 or ages 22–25. Industry-cluster inference, Holm correction, trend sensitivity, and an extended 2017 preperiod accompany the administrative models.

Controlled linked-CPS entry DID and event-study methods

The full inventory contains 115 genuine adjacent-month pairs and 95,862 jobless-origin transition opportunities at ages 22–25 from February 2017–October 2026. The main paired-outcome model uses 70,229 opportunities in the aligned 2019-start windows. These are repeated transition observations, not independent people. Exact origin age defines eligibility, including people who turn 26 before the next interview.

FT entry requires destination PEMLR 1/2 and PRFTLF 1. It includes wage/salary and self-employed work and is not restricted to first jobs or payroll hiring. The common denominator includes unemployment and nonparticipation. Destination THAIE defines the entry outcome, not treatment assigned to a nonworker. Prior industry is separately used as work-history information in the additive model. Unavailable occupations and histories remain explicit; they are not recoded as low exposure.

The proportional model uses PPML with state×group, destination-industry×group, state×month, industry×month, and group×season effects plus group-specific pandemic-quarter controls. The additive model regresses the paired Q5-minus-Q1–Q4 entry indicator on post and origin-state, seasonal, prior-industry and labor-force-history controls. Response models use only origin information with household-fold cross-fitting. Covariance alternatives and the distinction between pointwise inference and causal identification are reported with the results.

Quarter-specific event coefficients require four seasonal reference quarters in 2022 to coexist with group-specific monthly seasonality. State-plus-quarter covariance is indefinite for the full pretrend blocks and some event intervals. Those tests are withheld; plotted state-cluster intervals are conditional diagnostics. No interpolation enters the models. Detailed methods and limitations · Independent explicit-dummy numerical verification

Relative employment weakness is clearer than its mechanism

Using the same CPS current usual full-time definition for employment stocks and both transition endpoints, the Q5/Q1–Q4 employment ratio falls 9.31% between January 2019–October 2022 and January 2023–October 2026, with unavailable flow months excluded. The corresponding CPS comparison for the ACS calendar periods, 2017–2019 versus 2023–2024, is −8.73%. These are descriptive headcount comparisons, not employment-to-population rates or AI causal effects. The CPS full-time definition differs from the ACS past-year-hours proxy.

After inverse-response weighting, normalization by origin full-time employment, and equal weighting of calendar months, no individual pathway shows a clearly resolved additional Q5 deterioration. Exact arithmetic does not eliminate survey uncertainty

Across 115 observed adjacent-month pairs, 538,185 linked origin-age-21–26 person-transition records allow age-boundary movements to be separated from entry, exit, schedule changes, and recorded occupation changes. These are repeated records, not distinct people. Every monthly and period-mean accounting identity passes numerical checks. However, a sizable survey/coverage reconciliation term remains after matched flows and observed weight changes. In the primary integrated decomposition it contributes −22.60 additive log points, offset by other components, against a total relative change of −9.775 log points (−9.31%). This residual is not an economic exit, migration estimate, or AI displacement measure. Large offsetting contributions cannot be interpreted as causal shares.

Four weighting methods—design-only, inverse-response weighting, trimmed inverse-response weighting, and calibration to origin-only margins—and two age-boundary allocations are retained. Calibration does not force destination stocks to match. Conditional-ignorability assumptions, unobserved attrition, movers, occupation recoding, and historical mapping uncertainty remain limitations. The missing October 2025 survey is not imputed or bridged with individual transitions. The ACS-calendar flow sensitivity has 35 pre-period flow months because December 2016 was outside the verified cache, although all 36 pre-period stock months are retained.

Interpretation. Relative full-time employment weakness is reproduced, but fewer entry opportunities, increased exits, part-time displacement, occupational movement, and aging have not individually been demonstrated to explain it. This descriptive accounting does not replace the controlled entry DID or control differential pandemic recovery and industry demand.

Job quality, worker transitions, and hiring opportunities

Full-time and part-time counts describe hours categories, not contract permanence. Involuntary part-time status, realized earnings, and actual individual transitions are separate outcomes. The current regional job-quality estimates and attrition-adjusted youth transition contrasts do not establish AI-induced downgrading. SIPP offers longer histories but the high-exposure young sample is small. An absent significant transition is not proof that adjustment never occurs.

Archived job postings supply selected employer snapshots rather than a complete representative hiring panel. Missing archives are not zero vacancies. Posted pay is not realized pay, salary disclosure can respond to transparency laws, and few employers cannot support broad causal claims about hiring or job quality.

Discussion

Core conclusion

Relative weakness in high-exposure employment among adults aged 22–25 is the principal supported finding. Controlled entry models do not establish an additional Q5 decline, and the observed transition data do not show a rise in FT-to-PT moves. Wide intervals, pretrend differences, and selection limits prevent treating these findings as proof of either AI-caused harm or no AI effect.

Aligned CPS replication supports relative weakness, not a resolved mechanism. With current usual full-time status used consistently, the Q5/Q1–Q4 headcount ratio declines 9.31% in the entry-DID calendar comparison and 8.73% in the ACS-calendar sensitivity. The flow accounting does not isolate entry, exit, schedule substitution, recorded occupation movement, or aging as the explanation. Failure to resolve a pathway is not proof of no effect.

3. Why employment stocks, entry, and incumbent transitions can differ

Employment stocks reflect entry, separations, movements between occupations and schedules, aging, migration, and cohort composition. The completed CPS accounting now aligns the employment definition and target calendar periods within each comparison. It reproduces a 9.31% decline in the young Q5/Q1–Q4 full-time employment ratio, but every season-balanced, group-size-normalized pathway contrast has a conditional interval spanning zero. A negative accumulated entry contribution is not evidence of a post-2022 fall in the entry probability; existing replenishment differences and large offsetting flows also generate such accounting terms.

Mechanism attribution remains unresolved. A sizable survey/coverage reconciliation term remains, even with inverse-response weights, trimming, and origin-margin calibration. It must not be assigned to hiring losses, job exits, migration, or AI. Origin-age-21–26 linkage distinguishes movement into and out of ages 22–25, while symmetric age allocation provides a sensitivity check. Recorded occupation-band movements are not verified job or employer changes. State-bootstrap intervals condition on estimated weights and mapping, and do not capture full survey-design, first-stage, or nonignorable attrition uncertainty. See the aligned stock–flow results.

The sources also use different windows and populations. ACS compares annual cross-sections in 2017–2019 and 2023–2024, with past-year hours. The primary linked-CPS entry model compares destination months January 2019–October 2022 with January 2023–October 2026, using current usual FT status and ages 22–25 at origin. Someone who turns 26 can remain in the entry risk set while leaving the same-age employment stock. The earlier incumbent-transition readout uses a separate descriptive linkage/window. These outcomes cannot be treated as a single causal decomposition.

SIPP adds personal hours and income histories, but sparse young Q5 cohorts, survivor selection, and monthly-hours versus usual-schedule differences limit inference. Conditional earnings increases can reflect who remains employed, weeks and hours worked, or composition. They do not establish improved welfare for every exposed worker or measure the quality of all jobs offered.

4. Statistical limits and the paper's contribution

The additive entry DID is +0.962 pp (raw p=.009; two-test Holm p=.018), opposite to the proposed greater-Q5-decline hypothesis. It does not mean Q5 entry itself increased or that AI protected workers. Adding a differential trend gives +0.794 pp (p=.209); adding 2022 recovery terms gives +0.457 pp (p=.281); state-plus-year clustering gives p=.060. The proportional estimate changes from −9.81% without differential pandemic terms to +6.94% in the primary specification. Thus recovery and reference-period assumptions materially affect interpretation.

State-cluster proportional pretrend diagnostics give p=.386 in the primary window and .458 with a 2017 start; nonrejection does not establish parallel trends. The extended additive model rejects flat leads (p=.011; four-diagnostic Holm p≈.044). State-plus-quarter covariance is indefinite for the full pretrend blocks and some event terms. Invalid intervals and tests are withheld; plotted state-cluster intervals are separately labeled conditional diagnostics.

Original, IPW, trimmed, and calibrated weights yield proportional primary estimates from +6.46% to +6.94%, all inconclusive. This shows limited sensitivity to the tested observed-response adjustments, not immunity to attrition bias. Unobserved selection and movers remain concerns, and calibration cannot recover outcomes in cells without matched respondents. The working intervals do not fully incorporate CPS survey design, estimated propensity uncertainty, retrospective exposure measurement, or historical bridge uncertainty. Industry-month controls also change the proportional estimand to a within-industry comparison.

The separate controlled employment-stock model remains −6.60% (raw p=.069; 12-test Holm p=.756), with none of its primary age-by-outcome contrasts surviving correction. Its sensitivity and the administrative hiring checks provide context but do not replace the entry-flow test. The paper's contribution is to document relative occupational employment weakness and test whether entry and incumbent adaptation provide supported explanations, while keeping exposure, observed transitions, and AI causal effects distinct.
