Where does early-career employment decline as AI advances?

Graduate supply keeps rising at the master's and doctoral levels while the employment of new entrants weakens. This page tracks bachelor's, master's and doctoral output and employment from 2016, separates the COVID-19 period from the generative AI period, and projects each series to 2029. Korea is drawn in red in the cross-country figures, and new doctorates by age group are drawn in red tones in Figure 1.

Working note, version of 14 November 2026. Youngsam Chun. Descriptive evidence, not a causal estimate.

Research question and reading guide

The question is which strata of labour-market entrants lose employment as AI capability grows. Strata are defined here by degree level (short-cycle, bachelor's, master's, doctoral), by age band, by the AI exposure of the industry or occupation, and by country. Three periods are distinguished in every time series. The pre-COVID period runs to 2019, the COVID-19 period covers 2020 to 2022, and the generative AI period starts in 2023, the first full year after the public release of ChatGPT in November 2022.

What the figures show

  • By degree level. In Korea the decline is steepest for bachelor's graduates. Their employment rate fell from 66.3% for the class of 2022 to 62.8% for the class of 2024, while junior college and graduate school rates moved by less than one point. New doctorates show a slower but longer decline that accelerated in 2025, and the gradient by age is steep. In 2025 the share in work or with a confirmed post was 48.9% under 30 and about 60% for ages 30 to 39, against about 79% for ages 40 and over.
  • Young against old. Since 2023 the employment rate has fallen for bachelor's graduates, for new doctorates under 30 and for ages 15 to 29 as a whole, while the rate for ages 50 to 59 was flat to rising and the rate for ages 60 and over rose.
  • By age. The youngest entrants carry most of the fall. In October 2026 the employment rate of ages 20 to 24 was 3.6 points lower than a year earlier, against 0.5 points for ages 25 to 29.
  • By AI exposure. Losses of youth jobs sit almost entirely in AI-exposed industries, where employment of workers in their fifties rose. The United States payroll evidence shows the same sign for ages 22 to 25.
  • Supply. Bachelor's output is shrinking in Korea for demographic reasons, so falling employment rates at that level are not a supply glut. Master's and doctoral output is rising, which adds pressure at the top of the distribution.
  • Projection. If the generative AI period trend persists with damping, the employment rate of Korean bachelor's graduates reaches about % by 2029 and the youth employment rate about %.

Method

  • Periods. Pre-COVID 2016 to 2019, COVID-19 2020 to 2022, generative AI 2023 onwards. Trend lines are ordinary least squares fits within each period.
  • Missing values. Years between two reported anchors are filled by linear interpolation. Values recalled from secondary reporting without a primary table are also marked as estimates. All such points use hollow markers and are labelled in the tables.
  • Projection. A damped-trend extrapolation from the last observation, y(T+h) = y(T) + b × (φ + φ2 + … + φh), where b is the least-squares slope over the generative AI period and φ = 0.8. The band is ±1.28 × s × √h, with s the standard deviation of annual first differences, which approximates an 80% interval. The horizon is 2029, three years beyond 2026. Where official data end before 2026, the intervening years are projected as well.
  • Quarterly comparison. Korea uses the monthly Economically Active Population Survey; the United States uses the quarterly series of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York for graduates aged 22 to 27.

Limitations

  • The evidence is descriptive. Post-pandemic hiring normalisation, a preference for experienced hires, weak demand in accommodation and food services, and demographic decline act in the same period, and the Bank of Korea itself cautions against attributing the whole decline to AI.
  • A substantial share of the graduate-output points are estimates. The Korean degree counts and the 2016 to 2020 youth employment rates should be replaced with KESS and KOSIS tables before any citation.
  • Employment definitions differ across sources (graduate cohort surveys, labour force surveys, payroll data), so levels are comparable only within a panel.
  • The projection is a trend extrapolation with no structural model of AI adoption. It indicates direction under persistence and is not a forecast of policy outcomes.
